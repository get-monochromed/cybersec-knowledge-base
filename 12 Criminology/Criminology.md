# Criminology

## Summary

**Criminology** is the scientific study of [[Crime]]. Its central difficulty is explanatory: criminal behaviour is a vastly complex, sometimes incomprehensible phenomenon, and there is no all-encompassing *psychological* explanation for it — just as there is no all-encompassing sociological, anthropological, psychiatric, economic or historical one. It is unlikely that any single discipline can formulate basic "truths" about crime without help from other disciplines and well-designed research.

That framing is the useful part. Criminology is a meeting point for psychology, sociology, law, economics and history, and any claim that crime has *one* cause should be treated as a claim about the claimant.

## What it studies

- The definition and elements of [[Crime]] — act, prohibition, penalty, intent.
- Explanations of criminal behaviour: [[Classical Theory]] and [[Deterrence Theory]] on the free-will side; [[Positivist Theory]], [[Strain Theory]] and [[General Strain Theory]] on the determinist side; [[Conformity Perspective]] on human nature.
- What counts as a good explanation at all — see [[Scientific Theory]].
- Prevention and punishment, which is where the theories cash out into policy.

## Why simple fixes fail

Public officials, politicians, experts and the general public keep offering short, incomplete answers to crime — especially violent and street crime: more police, more cameras and better surveillance, armed teachers and more guns, sturdier locks, self-defence classes, stiffer penalties, speedy imprisonment, capital punishment.

Some of these work in the short term. The overall problem persists anyway, and crime rates themselves run in cycles — rising in some years and falling in others, which frustrates any explanation built on a single steadily-worsening variable. Interventions aimed at believed root causes — reducing economic inequality, improving educational opportunity, funding substance-abuse treatment — have considerable merit but demand public commitment, energy and money.

Part of the reason prevention stalls is cognitive rather than technical: psychological research indicates that most people have limited tolerance for complexity and ambiguity, and want simple, straightforward answers no matter how complex the issue.

[[Just-World Hypothesis|Just-world thinking]] is one concrete mechanism behind that preference: if the world is just, the offender's situation is self-inflicted and no structural explanation is needed.

## Relevance to security

Security work borrows from criminology more than it admits: situational prevention and CCTV are [[Deterrence Theory]] in practice, insider-threat monitoring is a [[Positivist Theory|positivist]] bet that antecedents and conditions predict behaviour, and fraud/financial-crime motive is [[Strain Theory]] territory. Getting the theory wrong produces controls that address a mechanism nobody is subject to.
