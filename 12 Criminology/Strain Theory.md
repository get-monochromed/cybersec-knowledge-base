# Strain Theory

## Summary

Merton's **strain theory** starts from the [[Conformity Perspective]]: humans are fundamentally conforming beings, strongly influenced by the values and attitudes of the society in which they live. Most members of a given society desire what the other members desire, and in many societies the accumulation of wealth or status is all-important — a symbol every member is expected to strive for.

The problem is distribution. Access to those goals is not equally available: some have the education, social network, personal contacts and family influence to attain them; others are deprived of the opportunity. Strain theory therefore predicts that [[Crime]] and delinquency occur when there is a **perceived discrepancy between the materialistic values and goals a society cherishes and the availability of legitimate means for reaching them**. Under those conditions a strain develops between the goals of wealth and power and the means of reaching them.

## What follows from strain

Groups and individuals experiencing a high level of strain are forced to decide between:

1. violating norms and laws to attain some of the sought-after wealth or power,
2. giving up on the dream and going through the motions,
3. withdrawing, or
4. rebelling.

This is the theory's real payload: the *same* social pressure produces different behaviour depending on how a person or group resolves it, so offending is one adaptation among several rather than a fixed trait.

> [!warning] Unverified
> The source paraphrases the model without naming Merton's adaptation modes (conventionally: conformity, innovation, ritualism, retreatism, rebellion) and without giving the year of the original formulation. The list above is the source's own wording, compressed into four plausible-sounding options; do not read it as the canonical five.
>
> The theory also rests on a *perceived* discrepancy, which is subjective — and the source offers no evidence on how that perception forms or how it is measured.

## Variants

### Strain among the wealthy (Messner & Rosenfeld, 1994)

Later strain theorists argued that crimes of the rich and powerful are explained by strain theory too. Even with greater access to legitimate means, these individuals have a continuing need to accumulate *even greater* wealth and power and to maintain their privileged status in society. Hence an already wealthy person may enter illegal schemes to hide income or evade taxes.

This extension is what makes strain theory useful outside street crime: the pressure is not deprivation but the maintenance of position, which never satiates.

### [[General Strain Theory]]

Agnew (1992) used *strain* in a different sense — strains as events and conditions disliked by individuals, of which blocked goals are only one.

## Relevance to security

- **Fraud and financial crime** by people who are not poor: the Messner & Rosenfeld extension explains motive where a deprivation model would find none.
- **Insider theft** often follows a perceived gap between entitlement and reward — see [[Insider Threat]]. The lever is the perception of the gap and the visibility of legitimate means (progression, grievance channels), not just pay.
- **Sanctioned insider recruitment** exploits the same gap deliberately; see [[Social Engineering]].
