# Crime

## Summary

Legally, **crime** is conduct — or a *failure* to act — that violates a law forbidding or commanding it, and for which a range of possible penalties exists upon conviction. **Criminal behaviour** is therefore behaviour in violation of the criminal code. To be convicted, a person must have acted intentionally and without justification or excuse.

That definition does three jobs at once: it names the prohibited act, it attaches a sanction, and it gates conviction on a mental element. The third part is what separates a crime from an accident, and it is where most of the interesting argument in criminology lives.

## The elements

| Element | What it means | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Act or omission** | Something done, or a duty left undone | Liability without an act is the exception, not the rule |
| **Prohibited by law** | Defined in advance by a forbidding or commanding rule | No crime without a prior legal prohibition |
| **Penalty on conviction** | A range of possible sanctions exists | The penalty range, not just its existence, drives [[Deterrence Theory]] |
| **Intent** | Acted intentionally | Excludes accident and, at the margins, negligence |
| **No justification or excuse** | Self-defence, duress, incapacity, mistake | The defences that negate the mental element |

> [!note] Terminology added by the editor
> The source gives the elements in plain English. Common-law shorthand for the last two is *actus reus* (the prohibited act) and *mens rea* (the guilty mind), with justification and excuse operating as defences rather than as separate elements. The Latin labels are not in the source.

## Free will as a legal assumption

Criminal and civil law are both built on the premise that individuals are masters of their fate — possessors of free will and freedom of choice. Punishment only makes sense if the offender *could* have chosen otherwise; the whole apparatus of intent, culpability and [[Deterrence Theory]] rests on that assumption.

The competing position is [[Positivist Theory]], which holds that antecedents — prior experiences and influences — determine behaviour and free will is largely undermined. The two schools are not reconcilable on this point, and most of the theories in this section pick a side.

> [!warning] Unverified
> The source states the free-will premise as a fact about criminal and civil law without argument or citation. Treat it as the standard textbook framing rather than an established jurisprudential consensus: strict-liability and regulatory offences, for instance, convict without proof of intent.

## Relevance to security

- Cyber offences are crimes in exactly this sense, so prosecutions turn on the same elements: the act, the applicable statute and the intent. Attribution establishes who acted; the mental element is a separate and often harder question.
- Insider cases are contested precisely at "justification or excuse" — authorisation, scope of role, and whether access was permitted but improper.
- Strict-liability regulatory regimes (data protection, breach notification) are the branch of law where intent is bypassed entirely, which is why fines land on organisations rather than individuals.
