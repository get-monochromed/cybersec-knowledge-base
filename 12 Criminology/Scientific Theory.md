# Scientific Theory

## Summary

A **scientific theory** is a set of interrelated constructs (concepts), definitions and propositions that present a systematic view of phenomena by specifying relations among variables, with the purpose of explaining and predicting those phenomena.

Applied to [[Criminology]], a scientific theory of [[Crime]] must do more than describe: it has to provide a general explanation that encompasses and systematically connects many different social, economic and psychological variables to criminal behaviour, be supported by well-executed and methodologically sound research, and use terms that are as precise as possible with clear, unambiguous meaning and usage — so that it can be meaningfully tested by observation and analysis.

## Verification and falsification

| Term | Meaning in the source |
|---|---|
| **Theory verification** | The process of testing a theory by observation and analysis |
| **Falsification** | The end result when a theory — or indeed any one of its propositions — is not verified |

The asymmetry matters for reading every other note in this section: a theory fails as soon as one of its propositions fails, so a theory that explains nine things and misses the tenth is not "mostly right". This is also why precise terminology is a scientific requirement rather than a stylistic one — a vague proposition cannot be tested, and so cannot be falsified or verified.

> [!warning] Unverified
> The source uses **falsification** to mean "the theory was not verified in testing", which is weaker and simpler than the falsificationist account in philosophy of science, where a single failed prediction refutes a universal claim while verification can never fully confirm one. The two senses are compatible in spirit but not identical; do not cite this note for the philosophy-of-science version.

## Models

A **model** is a graphic representation of a theory or a concept, designed to enhance understanding. Models accompany theories throughout the criminological literature; in this vault the same instinct appears in flow diagrams such as the [[Cyber Kill Chain]] and in [[MITRE ATT&CK]]'s tactic matrix.

> [!warning] Unverified
> A diagram is easier to read than a proposition set, and therefore easier to mistake for evidence. The source is explicit that a model is a representation of a theory, not a test of it.

## Why this note exists

Most disputes about criminal behaviour — and most disputes about attacker motivation in security — are disputes about whether a claim is testable at all. Holding the standard in mind is the cheapest available filter: name the variables, state the relation, say what observation would break it.
