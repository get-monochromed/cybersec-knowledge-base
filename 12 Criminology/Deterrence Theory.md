# Deterrence Theory

## Summary

**Deterrence theory** is [[Classical Theory]] in its modern form: the claim that crime is a choice, and that the threat of discovery or punishment can persuade a person not to make it. Both criminal and civil law are rooted in the belief that individuals are masters of their fate — the possessors of free will and freedom of choice.

## How it is supposed to work

- **By threat of discovery.** Surveillance cameras, ubiquitous on public streets and in places of business, assume that individuals choose to commit crime but may be persuaded not to by the threat of being caught.
- **By example.** A harsh sentence given to someone who has committed a political crime might discourage someone else from doing the same thing.
- **By punishment regardless.** Even if people are not deterred by the prospect of getting caught or a long sentence, they must still be punished, because the crime was an expression of their free will. Deterrence and retribution therefore coexist in the same theory: one aims at future behaviour, the other is justified by the past act alone.

> [!warning] Unverified
> The source asserts these mechanisms rather than evidencing them: no data on camera effects, sentence severity, or detection probability is offered, and no distinction is drawn between the offender deterred and the audience deterred. Both the empirical strength and the acceptable side effects (displacement, mass surveillance) are live research questions, not settled results.

> [!note] Terminology added by the editor
> The two audiences above are conventionally named **specific** deterrence (this offender, on this occasion) and **general** deterrence (others, by example). The source describes both without using the labels.

## Where the theory is weakest

Deterrence depends on three things holding at once: the offender believes there is a meaningful chance of detection, knows the penalty in advance, and is capable of the calculation at the moment of offending. Break any one and the threat does nothing. This is why the *certainty* of detection does more work than the severity of the sanction — a claim that follows directly from the pleasure/pain arithmetic in [[Classical Theory]].

## Relevance to security

- **Sanctions and prosecution** of cyber offenders, and the debate over naming-and-shaming, rest here.
- **Deterrence against states** is harder still: the returns from an intrusion are large, attribution is slow and often unprovable in public, and the retaliatory options are diplomatic rather than criminal. See [[Nation-State Actor]].
- **For insiders**, detection certainty — monitoring, logging, dual control — is the lever, not the disciplinary penalty in the handbook. See [[Insider Threat]] and [[Privileged Access Management]].
