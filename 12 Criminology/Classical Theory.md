# Classical Theory

## Summary

**Classical theory** holds that human behaviour is fundamentally driven by a choice, made by weighing the amount of pleasure to be gained against the amount of pain or punishment expected. Developed in the 18th century by the Italian philosopher **Cesare Beccaria (1738–1794)**, it treats *free will as the hallmark of human behaviour*, and it is the ancestor of almost every modern justification for punishment.

## The argument

1. People choose their behaviour, calculating pleasure against pain.
2. Punishment is therefore a price, and can be set to make offending a bad bargain.
3. To deter, punishment should be **swift**, **certain**, and severe enough to outweigh the expected pleasure.
4. If people knew in advance that severe punishment would be forthcoming, and soon, **regardless of their social status or privileges**, they would choose not to offend.

Point 4 is the radical part. Classical theory is egalitarian: the calculation is the same for everyone, so the response should not vary by who the offender is.

## Variants

- **[[Deterrence Theory]]** — the modern form. The same rational-actor model applied to prevention: surveillance cameras, harsh sentences, visible enforcement.
- **Rational choice / situational prevention** — the descendant position in security practice: raise effort, raise risk, reduce reward.

> [!warning] Unverified
> **The "severe" element is compressed here and probably overstated.** The source presents the triad as "swift, certain, and severe", but Beccaria's own treatise (*Dei delitti e delle pene*, 1764) is chiefly an argument for *proportionality* and *certainty* in punishment, and against excessive severity, torture and capital punishment. The textbook summary is a simplification; the certainty element, not the severity element, carries Beccaria's argument.
>
> Note also that the source gives no date for the treatise and no citation, so the edition and page cannot be checked from this vault.

## Tension with positivism

Classical theory and [[Positivist Theory]] are the two poles of criminological thought. Classical: behaviour is chosen, so punish. Positivist: behaviour is caused by antecedents, so intervene in the antecedents. Most real policy mixes both, incoherently — harsher sentencing (classical) alongside youth support programmes (positivist).

## Relevance to security

Every security control justified by "the attacker will see the risk and stop" is classical theory. So is every sanction regime: fines, prosecution, takedowns, notification-and-naming. The known weakness is the same as in criminology — if the probability of detection is low, raising the penalty changes very little, which is precisely the arithmetic of cyber [[Cybercriminal|extortion]] and [[Nation-State Actor|state-backed operations]].
