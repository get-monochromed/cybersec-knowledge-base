# Just-World Hypothesis

## Summary

Some people hold a personal theory that the world is a just place where one gets what one deserves — "if his parents had raised him right, he wouldn't be in jail." These individuals are called **just-worlders**: they believe things do not happen to people without a reason closely related to their own actions. The umbrella term for the family of hypotheses is the **just-world hypothesis**.

It matters in criminology because it is a *lay theory of causation*. It converts structural or situational explanations of [[Crime]] into moral ones, and so forecloses [[Strain Theory|strain]]-type accounts before they are considered.

## Evidence in the source

- People identified as just-worlders on the basis of scale scores have been shown to favour capital punishment and to be nonsupportive of many social programmes intended to reduce economic disparity between social groups.
- A **personal** just-world orientation ("I usually get what I deserve") is treated as adaptive and helpful for coping with dire circumstances. Dalbert and Filke (2007) found prisoners high in personal just-world orientation evaluated their prison experiences more positively and reported better overall well-being than those without it.

That second finding is the important subtlety: the *general* belief that the world is just and the *personal* belief that my own outcomes are deserved behave differently. The personal version looks protective; the general version looks punitive toward others.

> [!warning] Unverified
> The Dalbert and Filke (2007) finding is a single study, cited second-hand with no sample size, measure or effect size, and "high orientation" is defined only by scale score. Treat the protective effect as a hypothesis, not an established result.
>
> The source also asserts the capital-punishment and social-programme correlations without naming the scales or the studies beyond "the scales", so the direction of causation — punitiveness producing just-world belief, or the reverse — is untested here.

> [!warning] Unverified
> The source text contains a duplicated, truncated sentence ("Belief in a personal just-world ('I usually get what I deserve') is considered") that is reproduced here in corrected form. Flagged so the archive record and this note can be reconciled later.

## Why it matters operationally

Just-world reasoning reappears constantly in security and incident handling:

- **Victim blaming** after phishing or a credential compromise — "they should have known" — which suppresses reporting.
- **Resistance to systemic fixes**: if the breach was the user's moral failure, patching, MFA and least privilege look like optional extras rather than corrections of a design that invited the mistake. See [[Security Awareness Training]] and [[Social Engineering]].
- **Insider cases**: the assumption that a colleague's misconduct must have been visible and deserved lets an organisation skip the control-gap review.
